1. Direct Root Cause (Why Cell #7 Is Hottest)

Under your laminar flow condition (Re = 560), the thermal boundary layer progressively thickens along the minichannel cold plate as fluid flows from Cell #1 towards Cell #8. Because of this thermal saturation, the convective heat transfer coefficient (h) continuously degrades downstream—dropping by up to 42% before the coolant enters the exit manifold contraction zone . Cell #7 sits at the intersection where the fluid has absorbed cumulative heat from cells 1–6 and boundary layer thickness is near maximum, resulting in the thermal hotspot.

2. Impact on Pack Health & Degradation Risk

In your simulation, the intra-pack spread reaches ΔT = 17.6 °C (Cell #1 at 24.1 °C vs Cell #7 at 41.7 °C). Standard automotive battery engineering criteria strictly stipulate that pack temperature deviation must stay below ΔT ≤ 5.0 °C to avoid non-uniform electrochemical impedance growth and accelerated aging divergences between parallel cells . Operating indefinitely in this laminar state poses severe lifecycle penalties.

3. Literature-Recommended Engineering Mitigations

Peer-reviewed cold plate optimization studies propose two concrete interventions for this geometry:

  • Transition Regime Flow Rate: Accelerating coolant flow to transitional Reynolds numbers (Re ≈ 2,200 – 4,000) disrupts laminar boundary accumulation, reducing peak cell temperature by 4.8 °C with minimal pumping power overhead .
  • Dean Vortex / Rib Turbulators: Inserting dimpled or ribbed vortex generators adjacent to Cells #5–8 regenerates the thermal boundary layer locally, suppressing the hotspot without resizing fluid channels.
  • Counter-Flow Manifolding: Inverting alternate channel passages so cold fluid enters adjacent to Cells #6–10 flattens temperature variance across the pack .
